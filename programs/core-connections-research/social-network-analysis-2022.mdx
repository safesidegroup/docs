## Abstract

U.S. military suicides are increasing and disrupted relationships frequently precede them. This study examined whether the Wingman-Connect Program (W-CP) strengthened Air Force relationship networks and socially integrated at-risk members. Air Force training classes were randomized to W-CP or active control (cluster RCT), followed up at 1 and 6 months (94% and 84% retention). Participants were 1,485 Airmen in 215 technical training classes. Primary outcomes were social network metrics based on Airmen nominations of valued classmates after 1 month; baseline CAT-SS \>34 defined elevated suicide risk.

**W-CP increased social network integration, with the largest impact for Airmen already at elevated suicide risk** (_n_ = 114, 7.7%). For elevated-risk Airmen, W-CP improved all network integration metrics, including a **53% average gain** in valued connection nominations received (RR = 1.53) and **eliminated isolation**: no elevated-risk Airmen in W-CP were isolates after 1 month, vs. 10% among controls (_P_ \< .035). W-CP's effect on having ≥2 connections was still greater 2–4 months after classes disbanded. Wingman-Connect built enhanced suicide protection into unit relationship networks and counteracted the standard drift towards disconnection for at-risk Airmen — despite no explicit content targeting connections to at-risk Airmen specifically.

## Introduction

U.S. military suicides increased 50% from 2008 to 2018, and rates are now equal to the general population. Existing prevention focuses on identifying and treating those already suicidal — necessary but insufficient. W-CP is the first universal preventive intervention shown by RCT to reduce suicide risk and depression in a non-clinical Air Force population (Wyman et al., 2020), targeting the relationship networks military personnel are embedded within via a **network health approach**: (1) strengthening connections among group members, especially isolated ones, and (2) building each group's collective coping skills to create a healthy group culture.

This study tested whether W-CP achieved the objective of strengthening unit network structure over time, including socially integrating those at elevated suicide risk — an especially important test since **W-CP training has no content encouraging Airmen to form connections with at-risk peers**.

## Methods Summary

- **Network construction:** Airmen named up to 5 classmates they respect and would spend time with ("Valued Connections"), at baseline, 1 month, and 6 months.
- **Network metrics:** nominations received (in-degree), nominations made (out-degree), total nominations, ≥2 nominations made, isolate status, density of affiliation network, reciprocation rate.
- **Risk definition:** CAT-SS \>34 at baseline defined elevated suicide risk (114 Airmen, 7.7% of sample).
- **Analysis:** Generalized linear mixed-effects models adjusted for baseline network value, class random effect, gender, and group size; intervention-by-risk interactions tested differential impact for at-risk Airmen; addition/removal networks modeled dynamic connection changes.

## Results

### Baseline

Airmen at elevated suicide risk were directionally less integrated at baseline (fewer nominations received: 1.68 vs. 1.95; more isolates: 6.1% vs. 2.8%) and significantly more likely to have elevated depression (93.9% vs. 12.2%), loneliness, emotion regulation difficulties, and anger.

### Divergent Trajectories

In the **control** condition, at-risk Airmen showed a **19% decline** in total valued connections relative to non-at-risk peers (RR = 0.81, _P_ = .047) — confirming the expected drift toward disconnection. In **Wingman-Connect**, at-risk Airmen instead showed a non-significant trend toward _maintaining_ connections relative to peers (RR = 1.14, _P_ = .23).

| Outcome | Effect | _P_ |

Airmen at risk in W-CP made new valued-connection nominations at **2.49 times** the rate of at-risk Airmen in the control condition — the largest dynamic change observed in the study.

### 6-Month Follow-Up

Even 2–4 months after classes disbanded and Airmen transferred to new duty stations, W-CP trained Airmen were **33% more likely** to name ≥2 former classmates as valued connections (OR = 1.33) — comparable to the 1-month effect (OR = 1.40). Among controls, at-risk Airmen named 30% fewer valued connections than non-at-risk peers at 6 months; among W-CP trained, the gap nearly disappeared (only 4% fewer).

### Sensitivity Analysis

A robustness analysis (Frank et al., 2013) found that ~80% of the primary treatment effect would need to be attributable to bias to invalidate the inference — an implausibly high threshold, supporting a robust effect.

## Discussion

W-CP improved social network health for all Airmen, with the greatest benefit for those at elevated suicide risk — despite no content targeting at-risk Airmen specifically and despite broad baseline deficits (higher anger, depression, emotion regulation difficulties) among that group. One in 10 at-risk Airmen in the control condition were completely isolated from teammates just two months into training — a known suicide risk factor the current mental-health service paradigm is not designed to address. W-CP counteracted this drift by targeting the **unit's relationship network itself**, identifying small-unit relationship networks as a feasible, actionable, and modifiable target for universal suicide prevention. The mechanism appears to be more dynamic relationship formation: W-CP groups showed more additions _and_ removals of connections, with at-risk Airmen making three times more new nominations — suggesting the interactive training extended the natural process of network formation before it crystallized.

### Limitations

Effects were measured in relatively brief technical training classes; durability in already-formed, more heterogeneous units remains untested. Neither Airmen nor trainers were blinded to condition.

## Conclusion

This study supports the unique contribution of group-level interventions to improve social health across a military population while also ameliorating risk among individuals already at elevated suicide risk — without requiring individual risk identification. Additional longitudinal research is needed to test impact on suicidal behaviors over longer follow-up and to identify effective practices for scalable implementation.
