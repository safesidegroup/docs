## Abstract

**Purpose:** The structure of relationships in a social network affects the suicide risk of the people embedded within it. Although current interventions often modify the social perceptions (e.g., perceived support and sense of belonging) for people at elevated risk, few seek to directly modify the structure of their surrounding social networks. We show social network structure is a worthwhile intervention target in its own right.

**Methods:** A simple model illustrates the potential of interventions to modify social structure. The effect of these basic structural interventions on suicide risk is simulated and evaluated.

**Results:** Even an intentionally simplified intervention on social network structure (i.e., random addition of social connections) is likely to be both effective and safe. It had a high probability of reducing overall suicide risk, without increasing risk for those healthy at baseline, and frequently resolved stable, high-risk clusters.

**Conclusion:** Social network structure is a neglected, but valuable intervention target for suicide prevention.

## Introduction

The network of relationships in which people are embedded is intimately connected to suicide risk and protection. Starting with Durkheim's anchoring work linking density of social ties and suicide mortality rates, the importance of social networks has now been replicated in multiple populations and methodological approaches. Most current interventions explicitly address the social health of their recipients (e.g., perceived social support), but few directly target the structure of the natural social networks in which individuals are embedded — i.e., the characteristics of the surrounding network that transmit risk and protection.

This paper introduces **social network structure** (the pattern of relationships in a network) as an explicit intervention target for suicide prevention science: (1) social network terminology and the distinction between _social factors_ and _social networks_; (2) a simplified quantitative model showing that even simple structural interventions are likely to have protective effects; (3) emerging empirical evidence that structure is responsive to intervention and reduces suicide-related outcomes in randomized trials.

## Social Network Elements

Social networks are collections of people and their relationships. Networks have _content_ (attributes of people and relationships) and _structure_ (the pattern of who is connected to whom). Two networks can have identical content but distinct structures — and structure is capable of affecting suicide risk and protection over and above content.

By convention, people are _nodes_/_vertices_ and relationships are _ties_/_links_/_edges_/_arcs_ (terms used interchangeably). One edge attribute is always important — _directedness_: a _directed_ edge has meaningful asymmetry (parent-of-child), an _undirected_ edge is reciprocal (siblings, spouses).

## Social Networks vs. Social Perception

Traditional suicide prevention studies individuals' **social perceptions** (perceived burden, belonging, support) — an individual-centric approach. This paper studies **social networks** — the structure of relationships an individual is embedded within. Understanding isolation as a _perceived_ risk factor implies solutions that change a person's perceptions/behavior; understanding isolation as a _structural_ feature (missing ties) implies group-level solutions that repair or create ties. The two approaches are complementary, not competing.

## Social Networks and Suicide Risk

People at elevated suicide risk are (a) disproportionately clustered with one another and (b) less integrated into their broader networks — a pattern called **assortativity**, driven by three simultaneous processes:

1. **Social influence** — people emulate those they're already connected to.
2. **Homophily** — people form new connections with similar others.
3. **Shared environment** — proximity creates both connection and shared risk exposure.

These forces explain why suicide risk clusters: at-risk people influence friends toward similarity, form new ties mostly with other at-risk people, and share risk-elevating environments — while the reverse reinforces health among healthier peers. **Tendency toward isolation** compounds this: suicide-related risk is uncommon in the general population (e.g., \>90% of high schoolers have not attempted suicide), so at-risk individuals have few healthy replacement connections available once existing healthy ties dissolve, and the connections that remain available tend to be among others who are themselves more isolated.

## A Simplified Quantitative Model

The model begins with a random 10-person peer network; 3 people start at elevated suicide risk (dark), 7 at low risk (light). At each time step, every person adopts the majority state (≥2/3) of their connections from the prior step; with no majority, they stay the same. This simple rule reproduces both assortative clustering and tendency toward isolation seen in real networks. Different starting arrangements of risk within the _same_ network structure can produce entirely different outcomes — some resolve to total health, others to a stable peripheral risk cluster (Figure 2).

## Motivation for a Structural Intervention

Two suicide-protective tendencies emerge when most people in a network are already healthy:

1. **Most possible connections are health-promoting.** Because most people are healthy, most new ties either support an already-healthy person or nudge an at-risk person toward health.
2. **Healthy clusters are more durable than risk clusters.** A new tie between a healthy and an at-risk person is more likely to help the at-risk person than harm the healthy one, because the healthy person's broader friend group stays majority-healthy while the at-risk person's small circle can flip to majority-healthy with just one new tie.

This implies structural interventions are (1) likely to be safe even when untargeted, (2) likely to benefit those at greatest risk the most, and (3) **anti-clustering interventions** — capable of dissolving stable high-risk clusters that have already formed.

## A Sketch of Overall Effectiveness

Across all 32 possible single-edge additions to a stabilized at-risk network: **19% reduced total at-risk nodes**, 81% produced no change, and **none increased risk**. Adding three random edges simultaneously (4,960 possible combinations): **57% reduced suicide risk**, 43% no change, and only **0.2% increased risk**. Structural interventions are therefore both plausibly protective and very unlikely to backfire — even when nothing is known about who receives the new connections. Structural interventions can become **cluster-resolving interventions**.

## Empirical Support for Structural Interventions

The **Wingman-Connect Program** enhances early-career Air Force members' natural social networks through a 6-hour, 3-day group intervention — with no content instructing members to seek out at-risk peers. In a cluster-randomized trial (_N_ = 1,485), individuals at elevated suicide risk became progressively isolated in the active control condition, while those in Wingman-Connect gained social connections — at the end of training, 10% of elevated-risk control-condition Airmen were totally isolated vs. **0%** in Wingman-Connect. These network gains mediated reductions in suicide risk scores, depression, and occupational problems. Related interventions — strengthening classroom social integration, inter-generational school networks, and Youth-Nominated Support Teams — show similar promise, including reduced suicide attempts and mortality.

## Limitations and Open Questions

- The model is intentionally simplified and may poorly approximate complex real-world networks.
- It doesn't distinguish suicidal thoughts, attempts, and fatalities, which may respond differently.
- It assumes most network members are healthy — likely true in universal prevention contexts (schools, workplaces) but requiring more care in acute settings (e.g., psychiatric inpatients, where Youth-Nominated Support Teams augments connections to _trusted adults_ rather than potentially distressed peers).
- Open questions include how to durably induce new relationships, optimal intervention timing, and formalizing a theory of **network enriching interventions (NEIs)** for suicide prevention.

## Conclusion

Social network structure is a neglected but valuable suicide prevention target. Modifying network structure in even basic ways (e.g., adding random connections) can meaningfully shift risk across an entire network — a finding consistent with emerging real-world evidence from programs like Wingman-Connect. Suicide prevention would benefit from investing in social network structure as an independent intervention target in its own right.
